# Climbing prehab and performance review

Reviewed 21 October 2026 against the current plan in `public/data.js` and `exercise-review.md`. This is a focused literature review, not a registered systematic review. Searches covered climbing prevention trials, finger strength interventions, dynamic finger training, wrist/finger extensors and recent 2025-2026 work. Conclusions below distinguish measured outcomes from programming judgement.

Context: experienced boulderer around V7+, aiming toward V8, climbing Monday/Wednesday/Friday, cycling three days and doing supporting strength on Tuesday. Equipment includes dumbbells, a flat bench and a pull-up bar. A hangboard or lifting edge at home is not assumed. No current symptoms or injury history have been provided; the exercise doses below are for conditioning an otherwise healthy athlete, not treatment of an injury.

## Decision

Keep the two existing shoulder exercises. Keep supported wrist extension as the first optional forearm accessory to try. Do not make finger rolls, wrist curls, rotation drills, finger-opening bands or daily hangs compulsory. Nothing in the existing prehab selection needs removing for an otherwise healthy climber who tolerates it.

The useful changes are clearer progression, counting all gripping work toward recovery, and selecting any extra finger training for a demonstrated performance limitation. There is no convincing basis to promise that adding a particular finger or wrist drill will prevent climbing injuries.

If finger strength limits projects, a planned fingerboard or edge-lifting block is a stronger performance candidate than adding several general wrist exercises. Replace some demanding climbing with it. This is a conditional recommendation, not evidence that this climber needs more finger training.

## What the intervention studies establish

### Shoulder and elbow prevention: promising, but limited

A 2021 randomized study involved 84 male amateur climbers aged 18-19. A combined conditioning programme reported fewer shoulder injuries. Its population and multiple components limit transfer to an experienced adult boulderer or an isolated exercise. It cannot establish that two sets of external rotation plus scapular push-ups twice weekly reproduce the result. [Kozin et al., shoulder trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34846050/).

A related elbow report used the same 40/44 group sizes and a year-long intervention, with roughly 15 minutes of additional work at each of 3-4 weekly sessions. It included ring push-ups, supported pulling, eccentric forearm rotation and therapist-assisted movement work. Those components were not compared individually. Do not count the shoulder and elbow reports as independent replications, or attribute their results specifically to wrist extensions. [Kozin et al., elbow trial](https://physioquart.awf.wroc.pl/pdf-143832-103719?filename=Use+of+closed+chain.pdf).

A newer 2025 trial studied 30 elite Chinese national-team climbers over 16 weeks. Additional individualized medical training therapy improved several injury/discomfort and physical-test outcomes alongside existing care. Its rehabilitation context, combined treatment and unusual injury-event reporting do not validate a universal home prehab circuit or a particular wrist drill. [Qu et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39787782/).

Decision: retain sensible shoulder conditioning, with modest confidence about prevention and no percentage reduction promised for this exact routine.

### Fingerboard training: better evidence for strength than prevention

A 10-week randomized trial of 35 climbers found that twice-weekly fingerboard training improved selected climbing-specific force tests more than regular climbing alone. Actual climbing grades and injury prevention were not established by those outcomes. This supports a targeted strength block when finger capacity is a limitation; it does not prove that everyone needs one or identify a universally optimal dose. [Hermans et al., 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2022.888158/full).

### Finger rolls: useful loading option, uncertain climbing transfer

A 2024 randomized trial of 31 advanced-to-elite boulderers tested five weeks of dynamic finger-flexor training using dedicated equipment. Dynamic strength rose about 11.5% in the intervention group versus 5.3% in controls; the results section reports a significant difference in change. There was no demonstrated advantage in bouldering performance, isometric finger force or rate of force development. The paper's abstract and results do not report the dynamic-strength statistics consistently, so avoid overselling precision. Injuries were not the target outcome. This supports dynamic finger training as an option, with uncertain transfer, rather than proving that ordinary dumbbell finger rolls prevent pulley injuries. [Saeterbakken et al., 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2024.1461820/full).

Finger rolls primarily load the finger flexors, which climbing already trains. They are additional loading, not recovery work. A dumbbell roll also differs from the study's apparatus. Their practical attractions are adjustable load and a dynamic range of motion; neither establishes superiority to specific isometric edge training.

### Finger extensors: trainable, but injury protection remains a hypothesis

A 2023 study tested 78 climbers, with 52 participating in four weeks of assigned training/control conditions. The extensor-only group improved finger-extension strength by about 41%, with substantial variation. The study did not establish fewer injuries or better climbing performance. Its specialized resisted extension protocol is not equivalent to casual rubber-band opening. The proposed flexor/extensor ratio is not a validated injury-risk threshold or a target everyone should normalize. [Devise et al., 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2023.1243354/full).

Also distinguish finger extension, opening the fingers, from wrist extension, lifting the back of the hand. Evidence for one does not validate the other.

### Wrist loading: distinguish treatment from prevention

A small randomized trial of 21 patients found that adding eccentric wrist-extensor loading to standard care improved chronic lateral elbow symptoms and function. That is treatment evidence in symptomatic patients, not proof of prevention in healthy climbers. Outer-elbow, inner-elbow and wrist pain can involve different tissues and require different loading choices. [Tyler et al., 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20579907/).

The elbow climbing trial above included forearm rotation, but did not isolate its contribution. I did not identify persuasive controlled evidence that standalone wrist curls, reverse wrist curls or pronation/supination prevent climbing injuries or improve bouldering grades. Their use here is an optional capacity-building decision.

## Exercise decisions for this plan

| Exercise | Decision | Practical reason |
|---|---|---|
| Side-lying external rotation | Keep | Straightforward external-rotator loading with low grip demand. Progress it rather than keeping an effortless weight indefinitely. |
| Scapular push-up / plus | Keep | Reasonable serratus and shoulder-blade conditioning with an easily adjusted incline. Avoid forcing a large shoulder-blade squeeze or keeping the shoulders pinned down. |
| Supported wrist extension | Keep optional; first forearm addition to trial | Simple to dose with existing equipment. Start with one set and assess tolerance. It does not insure against elbow or wrist injury. |
| Wrist flexion curl | Optional for a specific capacity goal | More relevant if wrist flexion appears to limit slopers or compression, but that transfer is an inference. No need to add automatically to already substantial forearm loading. |
| Forearm pronation/supination | Optional alternative | Reasonable controlled rotation work when desired and tolerated. The prevention trial tested a combined programme, not this drill alone. |
| Finger rolls / curls | Optional finger-strength alternative | A way to load finger flexors dynamically. Do not add them as supposedly restorative work between hard climbing days. |
| Finger-opening bands | Low-priority optional | Can train extension when resistance is meaningful. No established injury-prevention benefit, protective ratio or grade improvement. |
| Hangboard / no-hang edge lift | Conditional performance option | Better sport-specific strength rationale if finger strength is limiting. A lifting edge can reduce shoulder demand but still loads fingers and pulleys. |
| Extra rows, pull-ups or scapular pull-ups | Keep optional | Useful if a pulling limitation is identified; not required to balance a climbing week. Their grip and elbow demands count. |
| Bench press / push-ups | Keep existing push slot | General pressing strength. No need for a fixed push-to-pull ratio or additional dips solely to prevent injury. |
| Mobility / stretching | Keep when useful | Use to address a movement restriction or comfort. Do not count it as established pulley or tendon protection. |
| Campus board / explosive finger drills | No prehab addition | These are demanding performance tools with no clear prevention role in this schedule. |

## A compact prescription

These are conservative starting doses chosen for the current week, not a trial-validated prevention protocol. Keep approximately 3-4 good reps in reserve for accessories. Muscles should work, while movement remains controlled and comfortable.

Tuesday after strength, and Friday after easy climbing:

1. Side-lying external rotation: start 1 set of 12-15 per side, build to 2 if recovered.
2. Scapular push-up: start 1 set of 8-12, build to 2. Use a bench or wall if needed.
3. Optional supported wrist extension: start 1 set of 12-20 per side on Friday only. After a couple of comfortable exposures, consider Tuesday too, then 2 sets if there is a reason to increase. Keep the forearm supported, use a relaxed secure grip and lower under control.

Rest about 45-60 seconds or longer as needed. Shoulder work alone is already scheduled at about 8-12 minutes; wrist work adds time. Omit it if the forearms are not recovering.

When the top of the range is consistently easy at the intended reserve across two exposures, add the smallest available load or make the bodyweight angle harder. Return to the lower end. Very light is a starting load, not a permanent rule. Do not increase load, sets and frequency together. If a dumbbell jump is too large for shoulder rotation, retain the load and improve control or use a smaller increment if available.

If trying forearm rotation instead, use one light set of 10-15 controlled turns each way once weekly at first. Support the forearm and keep the elbow still. Begin with a short lever; extending the lever increases torque substantially. Choose this OR the optional wrist exercise initially, so the response is interpretable.

If specifically choosing finger rolls later, start with one light set of 10-15 controlled reps after an easy climbing session, with several reps in reserve. Keep the wrist reasonably still, use a controlled finger range, and avoid letting the weight yank the fingers open. Build slowly if well tolerated. This is a familiarization suggestion, not the research protocol or a prescription for injured pulleys. A substantial finger-strength block should replace other finger work and use a defined performance goal.

## What to change about the week

Tuesday sits between hard Monday climbing and Wednesday endurance. Do not use that day to introduce heavy rolls, maximal hangs or a full wrist circuit. Friday is the better first trial for a small forearm accessory because the next climbing session is Monday, although Saturday cycling still loads the hands on the bars.

Count hard board attempts, fingerboard sets, rolls, optional pulling, RDL gripping and hanging abdominal work together when judging hand/forearm recovery. They are different exercises but share demands. Preserve the user's chosen hanging raises when tolerated; use the existing reverse-crunch alternative when grip fatigue limits them or Wednesday suffers.

Keep Friday easy, shorten sessions when performance deteriorates, and change one training variable at a time. These are practical load-management choices, not a proven protective weekly formula. No universal 10% progression rule or fixed rest interval guarantees injury prevention.

A prospective year-long study linked previous injury with subsequent upper-limb injury. Such observational associations help identify whom to assess carefully, but do not prove that a particular accessory prevents recurrence. A 2026 recreational-climber survey likewise cannot determine whether fingerboard training caused reported injuries: exposure, training errors, experience and reverse causation can confound the association. [Van Middelkoop et al., 2015](https://researchinformation.umcutrecht.nl/en/publications/incidence-and-risk-factors-for-upper-extremity-climbing-injuries-/), [2026 climber/provider survey](https://bmjopensem.bmj.com/content/12/2/e003239).

## Popular additions that do not earn a default place

Daily low-load hangs are interesting, but a 2024 Crimpd analysis was retrospective and self-selected. It measured strength changes rather than injury prevention or direct tendon/pulley adaptation. It does not establish that daily hangs heal or protect fingers, nor that adding them to this week is necessary. [Gilmore et al., 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-024-00793-7).

A 2026 randomized trial of 36 experienced climbers found that both low-load blood-flow-restricted training and conventional higher-load training improved finger capacities. It did not demonstrate fewer injuries or better bouldering grades, and does not justify introducing cuffs into this uncomplicated home programme. [Perrin et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41882800/).

Routine finger tape is also not a substitute for tolerable loading. A cadaveric failure experiment did not support circumferential taping as a prophylactic measure. That specific finding should not be extended to every tape method or clinically prescribed support during rehabilitation. [Taping failure-load experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11032223/).

## How to judge whether the changes help

Over 4-6 weeks, assess whether the chosen accessory becomes stronger with good control, whether climbing quality is maintained and whether symptoms remain absent. This period can assess feasibility and training response; being injury-free for a few weeks does not establish prevention.

For a performance block, define a repeatable relevant measure before starting, such as a standardized edge-strength test plus progress on representative projects. Avoid concluding that stronger wrist curls necessarily mean better climbing. Technique, movement selection, pulling capacity and project style can all remain limiting.

For healthy conditioning, do not push through new focal finger, wrist, elbow or shoulder pain. Reduce or stop the provoking load if symptoms or next-day stiffness increase. Persistent or recurrent symptoms need a climbing-aware clinician's assessment; an acute pop with swelling, bruising or lost function warrants prompt assessment. Existing pain would change these recommendations and should not simply trigger more prehab.

## Scope of this review

The exercise selections in the app already fit the evidence reasonably well. This review recommends no compulsory new accessory and no wholesale replacement of the current routine. It adds a clearer decision framework and optional trial doses. The subsequent scheduling update implements the separate Friday session described above. Saved exercise selections and weights remain compatible; no new media or deployment was needed.
